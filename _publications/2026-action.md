abstract: "We plan by considering multiple alternatives, yet only one action is ultimately performed. How memory later distinguishes executed actions from those that were merely considered, and what metacognitive confidence reveals about this distinction, remains unclear. Across two experiments, participants planned and executed routes through mazes before judging whether specific locations had belonged to the route they took or to an unexecuted 'path not taken'. Confidence was assessed either before or after route navigation. Confidence carried reliable trial-level information about the quality of route choice, indicating that it functioned as a genuine subjective signal about action-planning quality rather than as a global response tendency. Higher confidence was also associated with greater discriminability between executed and unexecuted routes in memory, indicating more distinct action representations, but showed no reliable relationship with the criterion used to endorse a memory. These effects were equivalent whether confidence was assessed before or after action execution. Together, these findings suggest that confidence indexes the precision of action representations during planning and that false memories for unexecuted alternatives arise from reduced representational distinctiveness rather than from changes in decision policy."
